Evidence A — What is body composition?

Body composition describes the different tissues and materials that make up the body. Four commonly discussed components are muscle mass, bone mass, body water, and fat mass. Each has important roles in normal body function.

Evidence B — Beyond the number

Weight and BMI do not describe every part of a person's health. They do not directly measure fitness, nutrition, sleep, mental well-being, blood glucose, or all aspects of body composition. No single number tells the whole story.

Evidence C — What influences health?

Health is shaped by many interacting factors, including genetics, nutrition, physical activity, sleep, stress, environment, access to resources, and medical conditions. These factors are not all fully under an individual's control.

Evidence D — Glucose takes a journey

When we eat foods containing carbohydrates, the body breaks them down into glucose. Glucose enters the bloodstream. The pancreas produces insulin, a hormone that helps glucose move from the blood into many body cells, where it can be used for energy or stored.

Evidence E — Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes

Type 1: An autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. People with Type 1 diabetes need insulin. It is not caused by eating sugar or by body size.

Type 2: A condition involving insulin resistance and/or the body not making enough insulin to meet its needs. Risk is influenced by multiple factors, including genetics, environment, and other health factors. It can affect people of different ages and body sizes.

Evidence F — Body composition and glucose

Muscles use glucose for energy. Physical activity and muscle contractions can help muscles take up glucose and can improve insulin sensitivity. Higher amounts of visceral fat are associated with greater insulin resistance, but body fat or body size alone cannot diagnose diabetes or determine someone's health.

Evidence G — Living with diabetes

Diabetes management is individual. It may involve checking glucose, insulin or other treatments, food choices, physical activity, and ongoing adjustments. Technology such as continuous glucose monitors and insulin pumps can support management. People with diabetes can live active, full lives.

Evidence H — Physical activity, exercise, and physical fitness

1. Physical Activity: Any body movement produced by skeletal muscles that uses energy. This includes everyday activities like walking, climbing stairs, gardening, and dancing—not just formal workouts.

2. Exercise: A specific type of physical activity that is planned, structured, and repetitive, done with the deliberate goal of improving or maintaining physical fitness.

3. Physical Fitness: The ability to carry out daily tasks with vigor, without undue fatigue, while having enough energy left for recreational activities. Fitness has multiple components.

Components of Physical Fitness

What does being fit actually include?

  • Cardiovascular Endurance: How efficiently the heart and lungs deliver oxygen during sustained activity.
  • Muscular Strength: The maximum force a muscle or muscle group can exert in a single effort.
  • Muscular Endurance: The ability of muscles to perform repeated contractions without fatigue.
  • Flexibility: The range of motion available at a joint; it can be reduced by inactivity.
  • Body Composition: The ratio of fat mass to lean muscle mass—one measure of what the body is made of.

Remember: physical activity includes everyday movement, while exercise is the planned and structured kind of activity. Both can contribute to physical fitness.

Important: This activity is for education—not diagnosis. Never guess whether someone has diabetes based on appearance or make assumptions about how they manage their health.
